# Four long-option setups worth testing, none yet proven

No setup in the research is proven to give positive expected value, net of Robinhood-style fills, for buying calls, puts or debit spreads in a $250 account. Four setups are still worth a disciplined test. They are ranked by how well the evidence and the account fit together. **(1) Oversold-dip bull call spreads, $1 wide, on SPY, QQQ or IWM.** The signal is well documented on shares, but no one has tested it through options. **(2) Pre-earnings long straddles or strangles, bought 3–7 days before the report and sold before the release.** This is the only event setup with peer-reviewed support: **+3.34% per 3-day trade** before costs in 1996–2010 data ([Gao, Xing & Zhang, JFQA 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v53y2018i06p2587-2617_00.html)). **(3) "Cheap volatility" straddles**, bought where implied volatility sits below realized volatility or where the implied-volatility term structure slopes steeply upward ([Goyal & Saretto 2009](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X09001251); [Vasquez 2017](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1944298)). **(4) IPO lockup-expiry bear put spreads**, which have stock-level evidence and no option-level test. Everything else a small account is tempted to do has documented negative or unmeasured expectancy for the buyer. That list includes holding through earnings, trading post-earnings drift with options, buying straddles into FOMC, CPI or NFP releases, buying 0DTE options, buying cheap out-of-the-money "lottery" calls, and gambling on FDA dates. The headwinds are large. Unconditional index straddles lose about **3% per week** ([Coval & Shumway](https://www.ssrn.com/abstract=239435)), and a retail round-trip spread of 5–10% of premium consumes most of any published edge. The account constraints add a problem of their own. A $50 cap per trade means a debit of **$0.50 or less per contract**. That excludes straddles on almost anything priced above about $10, and it makes the 20% per-trade cap roughly twice full-Kelly even for an optimistic edge. The honest recommendation is to paper-test these four rule sets on real historical bid/ask quotes, trade only those that pass, and treat the account as a laboratory rather than an income source. One caveat covers the whole report: the notes were built mostly from abstracts and search snippets, because primary PDFs and Robinhood's own pages were blocked. Verify key figures before relying on them.

## Option buyers start roughly 3% a week behind

Buying options unconditionally is one of the most reliably losing trades in empirical finance, and every setup below has to clear that drag before it earns anything. Zero-beta at-the-money S&P 500 straddles returned about **−3% per week** ([Coval & Shumway 2001](https://www.ssrn.com/abstract=239435)). Monthly S&P 500 put returns average about **−30% at the money, −60% six percent out of the money and −95% deep out of the money** ([Broadie, Chernov & Johannes 2009](http://www.columbia.edu/~mnb2/broadie/Assets/EOR_20080618.pdf)). A long 30-day variance swap lost **−66%** on average ([Carr & Wu 2009](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Variance-Risk-Premiums-Carr-Wu/ee61789ce93bff6274e17f46dbbd797b76a436e6)). Single stocks behave similarly outside event windows: straddles lose **−0.19% per day and −2.09% per week** ([Gao, Xing & Zhang working paper](https://www.ruf.rice.edu/~yxing/straddle_201305_03.pdf)). This premium is largest in index options and grows *when volatility is already high* ([Bakshi & Kapadia 2003](https://people.umass.edu/~nkapadia/docs/Bakshi_and_Kapadia_2003_RFS.pdf)). Buying options right after a VIX spike therefore means buying at the most expensive point of the cycle.

Two further costs fall hardest on a small account. The first is a **lottery premium**. Calls on the most lottery-like stocks underperform comparable calls by **10–20% per month** ([Boyer & Vorkink](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/8927/)), and the most out-of-the-money single-stock calls average about **−37% per month** ([Tinbergen WP](https://papers.tinbergen.nl/16022.pdf)). A $0.20–$0.50 contract budget pushes a single-leg buyer into exactly this corner. The second is the **bid-ask spread**. On SPX 0DTE options, retail effective spreads ran **6.1% of premium for calls and 4.9% for puts**, and about **60% of retail losses came from transaction costs** ([Beckmeyer, Branger & Gayda](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4404704); [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-04-21/day-traders-lose-358-000-per-day-gambling-on-zero-day-options)). Tick size compounds the problem: a $0.05 gap on a $0.40 option is 12.5% of mid. Only SPY, QQQ and IWM quote in **$0.01 increments for every series**, and other penny-program names get $0.01 ticks only below $3 ([MIAX Penny Program](https://www.miaxglobal.com/markets/us-options/all-options-exchanges/penny-program)). Robinhood's per-contract fees of about **$0.04 per side** ([RHF fee schedule](https://cdn.robinhood.com/assets/robinhood/legal/RHF+Fee+Schedule.pdf)) are trivial by comparison.

The spread cost can be cut. Traders who time their executions pay effective spreads **under 40% of conventional measures** ([Muravyev & Pearson, RFS 2020](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/33/11/4973/5732665)). The practical version is to work limit orders at or near mid and never hit the ask with a market order. IV crush is the last headwind. Implied volatility is *overestimated* immediately after earnings, so straddles bought after the announcement earn negative returns ([Chung & Louis 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927539816300743)). Anything held through an event pays for the crush.

## The one event edge with peer-reviewed support ends before the announcement

The best event evidence says the market *underprices the run-up* to earnings. It does not say the market underprices the announcement itself. Gao, Xing and Zhang found that at-the-money straddles bought 3 days before an announcement and held to the announcement date earned **+3.34%**, and a 1-day window earned about +2.3%, over 1996–2010 ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v53y2018i06p2587-2617_00.html); [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/volatility-effects/option-straddles-around-earnings-announcements/)). Chung and Louis found straddles bought before earnings earned **+5.1% over a month**, while straddles bought after earnings lost money. The pre-event edge was strongest when volatility *before* the event had been low ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/305385282_Earnings_announcements_and_option_returns)).

Three qualifications keep this from being a green light. First, the returns are **largest in small, volatile, thinly traded firms**, and transaction costs "drastically" reduce them ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327632662_Anticipating_Uncertainty_Straddles_around_Earnings_Announcements)). One summary puts the net figure at about **+1.64%** per trade after paying half the spread ([Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/www/Anticipating_Uncertainty-Straddles_Around_Earnings_Announcements.pdf)). Second, the Gao-Xing-Zhang window ends "at the announcement date." For companies that report before the open, that close already contains the reaction, so part of the +3.34% may be the announcement move rather than the implied-volatility run-up. Third, no peer-reviewed study replicates the result after 2015, which is the era of weekly options and heavy retail event buying. The one self-reported practitioner record weakens this year: SteadyOptions' pre-earnings book fell from triple-digit years to **+6.5% in 2025** ([SteadyOptions 2025](https://steadyoptions.com/articles/steadyoptions-2025-year-in-review-r822/)). That is a vendor figure and cannot be audited.

Holding through the report is a different trade, and it loses. In Option Alpha's test, AAPL straddles bought the day before and sold the day after won **41%** of the time and averaged **−1.31%**; for FB the win rate was 27% ([Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/podcast/long-straddle-earnings-option-strategy)). Options generally predict earnings-move size well, with fat-tailed exceptions ([Lipkin et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4701633)). A student paper reports **108% CAGR** from buying 1 day before and selling 1 day after, but it ignored commissions and slippage, used mid prices, and had single-week losses of 83.8% ([Khan & Khan, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4832160)). Treat it as noise.

Other events fare worse for buyers. Event-day variance premia are **large and positive, especially on FOMC days**, so option buyers overpay on average ([Wright, NBER w28306](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28306)). The FOMC premium in S&P 500 options averaged **45 bp** over 1996–2019 ([JFE 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X22000927)). Before FDA announcements, calls bought 5 days ahead earned about **15%** ([Bohmann & Patel 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jbfa.12600)). That profit tracks informed order flow, and retail traders cannot observe it in real time. Post-earnings drift has decayed toward zero ([Katz, Caltech](https://jkatz.caltech.edu/documents/28622/peads.pdf)), and in optioned stocks arbitrage can even reverse it ([Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/reversal-in-post-earnings-announcement-drift)). Lockups are the one remaining event with a predictable direction. Stocks fall around lockup expiry, and the highest idiosyncratic-volatility quintile falls **2–12% more** than the lowest ([JEF 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927539814000589)). No study tests whether put buyers capture that fall net of the put premium.

## Signal-driven buying works on volatility, not direction

The academic results that favor *buying* options are about buying cheap volatility, not about predicting direction. Goyal and Saretto sorted stocks on realized minus implied volatility. A long-short straddle portfolio, long where implied volatility looked cheap, earned **22.7% per month** with a Sharpe ratio of 0.71 over roughly 1996–2006 ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X09001251)). Vasquez sorted on the slope of the implied-volatility term structure and found **27.1% per month gross**, of which **14.8% came from the long leg**: straddles on the stocks with the steepest upward slope ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1944298); [CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/volatility-effects/exploiting-the-implied-volatility-term-structure/)). Straddle returns also show momentum over 6–36 months, with Sharpe ratios above 1.5 ([Heston et al., JF 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.13279)). All of these are gross, diversified, monthly portfolios of hundreds of stocks. A single straddle in one account holds all of the idiosyncratic risk that those portfolios diversify away, and the net-of-spread results could not be verified.

Directional signals have the opposite problem: strong evidence on shares and none on options. SPY RSI(2) dip-buying produced **181 trades with 82% winners and a 3.7-day average hold** over 26 years ([Guru Finance Insights](https://gurufinanceinsights.substack.com/p/i-backtested-the-classic-rsi2-mean)). A second test found **387 trades, 73% winners, a +1.8% average win and a −2.1% average loss** over 2000–2024 ([backtesteverything](https://www.backtesteverything.com/blog/rsi-mean-reversion-strategy-complete-backtest)). An RSI-below-30 plus VIX-above-20 trigger produced **57 trades and 86% winners** ([Backtest Substack](https://backtest.substack.com/p/when-spy-pulls-back-and-vix-spikes)). The edge is decaying, and the stock-level ConnorsRSI signal fell from **+1.5% to +0.3%** next-week return between 1990–2000 and 2015–2025 ([Concretum Research](https://x.com/ConcretumR/status/1879572346362593308)). A 2% move in SPY over four days is small next to the premium of a two-week at-the-money call. Losing trades held about 10 days also bleed theta. The notes found **no credible published backtest** of these signals expressed through calls or call spreads with realistic costs.

One piece of Cboe-published evidence cuts the other way. Cboe customers' **call purchases** were profitable in both sample periods, while put purchases were not ([Cboe](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/education/research_publications/Retail_Profitability.pdf)). Cboe profits from retail volume, and that sample covers a strong 2020–2023 bull market, so read it as beta rather than skill. The larger point is sound: **long puts are the worst long-option trade**. Index put buying has a long-run Sharpe of about **−1.1 before costs** ([AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/White-Papers/Chasing-Your-Own-Tail-Risk-Revisited)), and put protection often worsens drawdown per unit of return ([Israelov, AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/White-Papers/Pathetic-Protection-The-Elusive-Benefits-of-Protective-Puts)).

Debit spreads help at the margin. Returns grow sharply more negative as strikes move out of the money ([Broadie et al.](http://www.columbia.edu/~mnb2/broadie/Assets/EOR_20080618.pdf)). Selling the further-OTM leg therefore sells the more overpriced option in a put spread or in a single-stock call spread. It also cuts the vega exposed to IV crush. A spread creates no edge on its own, though. Unconditional 0DTE directional spreads have outcome ranges "often symmetric around zero," and many structures that are positive gross turn **negative net** after half-spread execution ([Vilkov et al.](https://github.com/vilkovgr/0dte-strategies/blob/main/docs/paper/paper-annotated.md)). Retail 0DTE traders lost about **$358,000 per day** on SPX, with buyers doing worst ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-04-21/day-traders-lose-358-000-per-day-gambling-on-zero-day-options)). A trader-level study finds much smaller per-trade losses ([Bogousslavsky & Muravyev](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4682388)), but no study finds 0DTE buying profitable.

## A $50 loss cap forces $1-wide ETF spreads and sub-$10 stocks

The account arithmetic decides which structures are even available. One contract controls 100 shares, so the **$50 per-trade cap (20% of $250) means a debit of $0.49 or less**. The **$75 total cap (30%)** allows one full-size position plus at most one of about $25. The rule of thumb that an ATM straddle costs about 0.8 × price × IV × √(time) shows how tight this is (my calculation). A 7-day SPY straddle at 15% IV costs about **$10.80, or $1,080**. A 7-day straddle on a $10 stock at 50% IV costs about **$0.55**. Earnings inflate implied volatility, so pre-earnings straddles fit the cap **only on stocks priced below about $8–10**. Those names tend to have wider percentage spreads and nickel ticks above $3. That is the same illiquid corner where Gao-Xing-Zhang found the edge largest and costs heaviest. The setup with the best evidence therefore fits the account worst.

**$1-wide verticals on SPY, QQQ and IWM** fit cleanly. A near-the-money $1 spread costs roughly $0.40–$0.60 and quotes in pennies on both legs. It behaves almost like a binary bet that the ETF finishes above (or below) the strikes. That property makes the trade easy to evaluate: the debit divided by the width is roughly the market's implied probability of success. The spread has positive expected value only if the signal's hit rate beats that number by more than the round-trip cost. Even two-cent legs cost around 8% of a $0.50 debit round trip. Standard theory also suggests the real-world probability of an index rising over a few days exceeds the risk-neutral probability built into call-spread prices, because of the equity and variance risk premia (my inference). That inference is consistent with Cboe's call-buying finding, but no study measures it for these spreads, and the test below exists to measure it.

The sizing is aggressive, and the account cannot size down. The Kelly fraction for a 1:1 spread that wins 55% of the time is 10%; half-Kelly is **$12.50**, and one contract usually costs more than that. At the 20% cap, **five straight maximum losses at a fixed $50 wipe out the account**. With a 50% loss rate, a 5-loss streak occurs somewhere in 50 trades about **55%** of the time, and at a 60% loss rate about 83% of the time (my simulation). Robinhood's mechanics add expiration risk. The firm auto-exercises options that are $0.01 or more in the money only if the account has the buying power. Otherwise it tries to sell at-risk positions from about **3:30 PM ET** on a best-effort basis ([Robinhood support](https://robinhood.com/support/articles/360001214723/expiration-exercise-and-assignment/)). A $250 account cannot exercise an in-the-money SPY call, and a pinned spread could leave a stock position many times the account's size. Every rule below therefore exits before expiration-day afternoon. Options settle T+1, so a cash account must avoid good-faith violations ([Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/trading-investing/trading/avoiding-cash-trading-violations)). FINRA's replacement of the pattern-day-trader rule takes effect June 4, 2026, with implementation phasing in through October 2027 ([FINRA RN 26-10](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/notices/26-10)). Robinhood's timeline for applying it is unconfirmed.

## Four rule sets, fixed before testing

All four setups use a **15:45 ET quote snapshot** for signals and fills, following ORATS' practice of sampling about 14 minutes before the close to avoid stale closing quotes ([ORATS](https://orats.com/university/backtesting-methodology)). All take **one contract**, skip any entry whose modeled debit exceeds **$0.49**, and hold at most one A/B/C/D position at a time, which keeps total risk within the $75 cap. Parameters outside the listed grid may not be tuned.

| ID | Setup | Exact entry | Structure | Exact exit | Grid (every cell logged) | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Oversold-dip bull call spread on SPY / QQQ / IWM | At 15:45, the underlying's price is above its 200-day SMA and RSI(2), computed with the 15:45 price as the day's close, is below the threshold. | Expiry: the first one at least N trading days out. Long call: the highest strike at or below spot. Short call: long strike + $1. | The first of: RSI(2) above 70 at 15:45; price above the 5-day SMA; 15:45 on the trading day *before* expiry. No stop, because risk is defined. | RSI < {5, 10}; N = {5, 10}; VIX > 20 filter {on, off}. **8 cells.** | Shares only (73–86% wins); **no option-level test exists** |
| **B** | Pre-earnings long straddle | At 15:45 on T−k trading days before the reaction session; announcement date and BMO/AMC timing confirmed. The stock's ATM straddle has a spread of 10% of mid or less and ATM open interest of 500 or more. | Expiry: the first weekly after the reaction session. ATM straddle, or a strangle one strike out on each side. | 15:45 on the last session before the reaction: the announcement day for after-close reporters, the day before for pre-open reporters. **Never hold through.** | k = {3, 5, 7}; straddle vs strangle; filter "mean abs move over last 8 announcements ÷ current implied move > 1" {on, off}; filter "20-day realized vol below its 1-year median" {on, off}. **24 cells.** | Peer-reviewed, gross, 1996–2010; untested post-2015; fits the cap only for stocks under ~$10 |
| **C** | Cheap-volatility straddle | At 15:45 on the first trading day after monthly expiration, rank liquid optionable stocks on (trailing realized vol − 30-day ATM IV) and on IV slope (about 90-day IV minus 30-day IV). Buy the top-ranked name that is in the top decile on both, has no earnings before expiry, and has a straddle spread of 10% of mid or less. | ATM straddle in the next monthly expiry. | 15:45 on {10 trading days after entry, or the day before expiry}. | Realized-vol window {1 month, 12 months}; exit {2 options}; both filters vs slope-only. **8 cells.** | Long leg of Goyal-Saretto and Vasquez portfolios, gross; single-name version untested |
| **D** | Lockup-expiry bear put spread | At 15:45, five trading days before the IPO lockup expiry. The stock has listed options and is in the top tercile of 60-day idiosyncratic volatility among IPOs with lockups that month. | Expiry: the first one at least 10 trading days after the lockup. Long put: ATM. Short put: the next listed strike down. | 15:45 five trading days after the lockup date, or the day before expiry, whichever comes first. | Entry {T−5, T−10}; spread width {one strike, two strikes}. **4 cells.** | Stock-level only; **no option study** |

The total grid is **44 configurations**, under the rough ceiling of about 45 independent trials that five years of data can support before a Sharpe of 1 appears from noise alone ([Bailey et al., Notices of the AMS](https://www.ams.org/notices/201405/rnoti-p458.pdf)). Setup A leads for this account. It is the only one that fits the cap on penny-quoted underlyings without distortion, and its break-even can be measured directly by comparing the realized hit rate with debit ÷ width. Setup B has the strongest literature, but the cap confines it to low-priced stocks where the edge is most eroded by costs. For research, run B on the full liquid universe with returns stated as a percent of premium, then report the sub-$0.49 subset separately. Setup C is a research bet on a portfolio anomaly squeezed into one position at a time. Setup D is included because it is the only remaining event with a documented stock-level direction, and it is the most likely to fail.

## Testing on real quotes needs pessimistic fills and a null that can fail them

The test must use historical option **bid and ask** quotes. Black-Scholes prices with VIX as the volatility input ignore skew and spreads, and those are exactly the costs that sink option buyers. For Setup A, free end-of-day to minute-level chains for SPY, QQQ and SPX from 2010–2023 are available from OptionsDX ([Arias, CFA](https://davidariasfinance.com/scripts/free-options-data/)). IWM is not on that list, so it needs a paid source. Setups B, C and D need a survivorship-free cross-section of single-stock options. That means OptionMetrics IvyDB through WRDS, ORATS, Cboe DataShop/LiveVol, Polygon (Massive) or Databento ([Alphanume overview](https://www.alphanume.com/blog/best-options-data-providers-for-systematic-trading-research); [LiveVol](https://www.livevol.com/stock-options-analysis-data/)). Include delisted underlyings. Robinhood's option historicals are useful for spot-checking recent fills, but the notes could not confirm that they cover expired contracts.

Model fills pessimistically, because a strategy that only works at mid is not tradeable. Use ORATS' convention of paying about **75% of the bid-ask width** on single legs, falling to about **53% for four-leg orders** ([ORATS](https://orats.com/university/backtesting-methodology)). Two-leg spreads fall between those; 65% is a reasonable interpolation (my assumption). Add $0.04 per contract per side, and rerun at mid and at full width. Apply the $0.49 debit cap *after* modeling the fill. Sample size is the binding constraint. Long-option returns have a standard deviation near 100% of premium, so detecting a +10% per-trade edge at two standard errors needs about **400 independent trades**, from (2 × 100 / 10)² (my calculation). Overlapping trades on the same dates do not count as independent ([Nasdaq on backtesting pitfalls](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/avoid-these-7-options-backtesting-pitfalls-2019-07-01)). Setup A will generate perhaps a few hundred signals across three highly correlated ETFs in 2010–2023, and far fewer independent ones. It cannot reach statistical proof on its own history. Its test must instead require consistency across ETFs and sub-periods.

Split the data chronologically. Develop on 2010–2018, test once on a 2019–2023 hold-out that contains March 2020 and the 2022 bear market, then paper-trade on live Robinhood quotes. Treat a failed hold-out as spent: after changing the rules you cannot reuse it. Acceptance requires all of the following. First, mean net P&L per trade is above zero at 65–75% fills, with a block-bootstrap lower bound above zero. Second, the strategy beats a **random-entry null** that uses the same structure, days to expiry and exits but enters on random days. This test matters most for A, because unconditional bull call spreads in a rising market can look profitable with no signal at all. Third, results hold in both halves of the sample and at twice the costs. Fourth, the Deflated Sharpe Ratio computed with all 44 logged trials exceeds 0.95 ([Bailey & López de Prado, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2460551)). Setup B needs one more check: separate BMO and AMC reporters, so that no announcement reaction leaks into the pre-event return.

## Most popular option-buying plays have no or negative evidence

The honest ledger is lopsided. **Documented negative for buyers:** holding straddles or directional options through earnings in large caps; buying after earnings to ride drift; straddles into FOMC and NFP; retail 0DTE buying; deep out-of-the-money "lottery" calls; and routine put buying for protection. All are cited above. **No option-return evidence at all:** PDUFA and trial-readout straddles, stock-split calls, index-inclusion plays, CPI straddles, and lockup puts (Setup D exists to test the last one). The academic work shows options already price splits and inclusion ([JBF 2008](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378426607002804); [EFMA 2022](http://www.efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2022-Rome/papers/EFMA%202022_stage-3032_question-Full%20Paper_id-329.pdf)). **Evidence exists, but only in a form the account cannot replicate:** FDA call buying, which depends on seeing informed order flow, and the long-short volatility portfolios behind Setup C. **Signals proven on shares but never through options:** RSI(2), Double-7s and VIX-spike dip buys, which underlie Setup A. Even the best-supported setup, B, rests on pre-2011 data, and no one has shown that it survives full retail spreads.

## Conclusion

The research turns the question around. The strongest evidence about buying options is evidence about *when not to*: after events, into macro releases, far out of the money, and at the ask. The remaining edges are narrow gaps in timing or relative value, a few percent per trade, the same size as the spread. That makes execution quality (limit orders at mid, penny-quoted underlyings, exits before expiration-day afternoon) as important as the signal. The $250 account adds an irony. Its cap pushes it toward cheap contracts on cheap stocks, where the structural drag is heaviest. The one structure that fits it well, the $1-wide ETF vertical, happens to be the most transparent to evaluate, because its price states the implied probability it has to beat.

The practical implication is to reverse the usual order. Run the 44-cell test first on real quotes and paper-trade the survivors. Commit capital only to a setup whose realized hit rate clears debit ÷ width plus costs out of sample. Until one does, the expected-value-maximizing position size for any of these trades is zero. If one passes, the 20% cap is still too large for an unproven edge, and trading less often is the only way this account can size down.
